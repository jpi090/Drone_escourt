# Drone Escorts — Investor Pitch Deck

**Version:** 1.0 · **Date:** 5 October 2026 · **Stage:** Seed, $2.5M
**Format:** 14 slides. Each slide below gives the on-screen content, then speaker notes.
Target run time: 12 minutes, leaving 18 for questions.

> **How to use this.** The on-screen content is deliberately sparse — short lines, one number per
> point. The speaker notes carry the argument. Resist the urge to put the notes on the slides.
> All figures trace to `BUSINESS-MODEL.md`; all capability claims trace to `SRS.md`. If an investor
> pushes on a number, open the model rather than improvising.

Seed round · $2.5M · [Founder name, contact]

> **Speaker notes.** Keep this to fifteen seconds. Say the one-liner, say the round size, move on.

## Slide 2 — The problem

A convoy leaves at 6am with $400,000 in cargo.

The crew knows the route. **So does everyone else.**

- Ambush points on fixed routes are known and repeated
- Ground escort sees 50 metres; the threat is at 500
- When something happens, there is no evidence and no warning — only a report afterwards

> **Speaker notes.** Lead with the logistics scenario, not a consumer one. It is concrete,
> commercially rational, and it tells the investor immediately that this is a B2B business with a
> consumer upside rather than a consumer gamble.
>
> The key insight to land: the problem is not a lack of security personnel. It is a lack of
> **altitude**. A ground escort has no horizon. Everything we sell follows from that sentence.
>
> If the room is Nigerian or African, the kidnap-for-ransom and cash-in-transit context needs no
> explanation. If the room is not, spend one extra sentence on it — but do not dwell, and do not
> let the deck become a tour of other people's tragedies. State the market need factually.

## Slide 3 — What we do

Press one button. A drone launches from the vehicle and escorts the journey.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sees ahead** | Live aerial view of the road 500m out |
| **Stays with you** | GPS follow-me, hands-free, for the whole route |
| **Raises the alarm** | SOS reaches a staffed operations centre in under 10 seconds |
| **Remembers** | Court- and insurer-grade evidence record of every mission |

> **Speaker notes.** Four verbs. Do not list features — the master spec has forty and reciting them
> makes the product sound unbuilt. These four are what the customer buys.
>
> Say plainly: **the drone observes and records. It never intervenes.** Saying this unprompted
> builds enormous credibility, because the sophisticated investor is already wondering whether you
> have thought about liability. You have.

## Slide 4 — Demo

*[Live prototype — 90 seconds]*

Launch → escort in flight → advisory raised → SOS → operator response → mission record

> **Speaker notes.** Run the prototype. Do not narrate every click; let it play. Hit these beats:
>
> 1. Tap Launch Escort — pre-flight checks run and the drone lifts
> 2. The drone follows the vehicle along the route, telemetry live
> 3. An advisory appears ahead on the route, with its source shown
> 4. Press SOS — the operator board lights up, contacts notified, timer running
> 5. Operator acknowledges; the mission record is written
>
> Say once, clearly: **"the flight is simulated; the software is real."** Never let an investor
> discover that themselves. Stating it costs you nothing and conceals nothing; being caught
> costs you the round.

## Slide 5 — Why now

Three curves crossed in the last 24 months:

- **Airframes** — enterprise drones with docking, 40-minute endurance, obstacle avoidance, at a
  fraction of 2020 cost
- **Connectivity** — 5G coverage on commercial corridors makes live video from the air routine
- **Regulation** — civil aviation authorities are issuing route-specific commercial authorisations
  instead of blanket refusals

The hardware stopped being the hard part. **The operating licence and the network became the
hard part** — and those are the assets we are building.

> **Speaker notes.** This is the slide that separates you from the ten other drone pitches the
> investor has seen. Most of those pitch the aircraft. You are pitching the regulatory and
> physical network around it, which is the part that is actually defensible.

---

## Slide 6 — The honest constraint

We cannot legally fly a drone that autonomously follows anyone, anywhere, today.

**So we did not design that product.**

| | Phase 1 — now | Phase 3 — with authorisation |
|---|---|---|
| Flight | Visual line of sight, licensed pilot supervising | Autonomous lock-on |
| Launch | Fixed and vehicle docks on approved corridors | City-wide dock network |
| Routes | Per-corridor authorisation | Open operating area |
| **Gross margin** | **12%** | **43%** |

> **Speaker notes.** Put the constraint on screen before anyone asks. This slide does more work
> than any other in the deck for three reasons:
>
> 1. It proves you understand the regulatory landscape, which most drone founders do not.
> 2. It reframes regulation from a risk into a **moat** — every corridor we clear is an
>    18-month delay for a competitor.
> 3. It sets up the margin story on the next slide.
>
> If someone asks "what if the regulator never grants autonomy?" — the answer is that the Phase 1
> product is sold, priced, and economically viable at 34% margin without it. Autonomy is upside,
> not the thesis. Have that sentence ready verbatim.

## Slide 7 — The business we are actually in

Margin is gated by exactly two variables.

| Scenario | Utilisation | Drones per pilot | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Launch | 55% | 4 | **12%** |
| Maturity | 80% | 4 | **34%** |
| With autonomy | 80% | 8 | **43%** |

**Autonomy is not a feature. It is the margin.**

Every phase of our roadmap is one step up this curve.

> **Speaker notes.** This is the centre of the pitch. Everything else supports it.
>
> Most founders present a roadmap as a list of features they would like to build. You are
> presenting it as a sequence of moves against two measurable variables. That is the difference
> between a product plan and a thesis, and investors fund theses.
>
> Be ready for: "12% gross margin is terrible." Correct answer: "Yes — at launch, on owned
> hardware. Which is why owned hardware is not our growth engine." Then go straight to slide 8.

## Slide 8 — The asset-light engine

One security-company partner, 20 drones, **zero capex to us:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Revenue / month | $2,400 |
| Direct cost | $550 |
| **Gross margin** | **77%** |

That single account out-earns **eleven owned drones** at launch utilisation.

They bring capital, pilots, and the customer. We bring the platform.

> **Speaker notes.** This is the lean-forward slide. Deliver the comparison slowly and let it land.
>
> The strategic conclusion falls straight out of the arithmetic: we are not building a fleet
> operator, we are building the operating system that fleet operators run on. We own enough
> aircraft to serve direct accounts, prove the model, and demo — not enough to be a capital sink.
>
> It also answers the competitive question before it is asked: incumbent security firms are not
> our competitors, they are our highest-margin channel.

## Slide 9 — Beachhead and expansion

**Start:** cash-in-transit and high-value logistics

- Already budget for escort — we displace a cost line, we do not create one
- **Fixed, repeated routes** — authorisation is obtainable per corridor
- Owned fleets — docks installable at scale
- Losses measured in currency — ROI arguable with a spreadsheet
- One contract puts 40 docks in the air

**Then:** executive protection → security-company white-label → consumer and family

> **Speaker notes.** Explain explicitly why consumer is not first, because the investor will assume
> the consumer app is the business. Forty consumers put forty docks in forty driveways with forty
> sales cycles and forty regulatory footprints. One logistics contract puts forty docks on one
> corridor. The physics of distribution decide the order.
>
> Consumer is the eventual volume story and the brand. It is not the launch motion.

---

## Slide 10 — Moat

In order of how hard each is to copy:

1. **Corridor authorisations** — slow, relationship-driven, durable
2. **Dock density** — escort value is a function of response time; density compounds
3. **Insurance partnership** — turns the product into a premium discount, and the insurer into a channel
4. **Evidence chain of custody** — accepted by insurers and courts means we become the system of record
5. **Safety record** — hours flown without incident; cannot be bought

Deliberately **not** on this list: our AI models. Computer vision is commoditising. Detection
quality will not be the differentiator.

> **Speaker notes.** The last line is the most persuasive thing on the slide. Every competing deck
> claims proprietary AI as the moat. Saying out loud that CV is commoditising — and that the real
> assets are regulatory and physical — demonstrates that you have thought one layer deeper than
> the field.
>
> The insurance point deserves a sentence of its own: if escorted cargo measurably reduces loss,
> the insurer prices it, and the product becomes something the customer **saves** money by buying.
> That is the strongest commercial position available to this business.

### Three questions to have memorised

**"What if the regulator says no to autonomy?"**
The Phase 1 product is viable at 34% gross margin without it. Autonomy is upside, not the thesis.

**"You are selling protection you cannot guarantee."**
We sell observation, early warning, and evidence. We say so contractually, in onboarding, and in
the product. We never promise intervention, and the drone never intervenes.

**"Why you?"**
Answer honestly with your actual background and name the gap you are hiring for. A founder who
names their gap is more fundable than one who pretends there is none.
